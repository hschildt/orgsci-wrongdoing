# Strain and performance pressure

**Core idea.** Wrongdoing starts when valued goals outrun the legitimate means of reaching them. Merton (1938) called the adaptation "innovation": keeping the goal while abandoning approved means. Vaughan (1983) moved the argument to organizations, which face scarcity and competition and so have structural reasons to cut corners. Later work shows that specific stretch goals, rewards tied to thresholds and performance below aspirations all raise the likelihood of misconduct.

**What this lens explains.** Motive and timing: why the deviant path was taken, and when. Code the pressures and the choice, not how the practice was later sustained (that belongs to normalization) or how individuals justified it (moral disengagement).

**Key references.** Merton 1938; Vaughan 1983; Agnew 1992; Schweitzer, Ordóñez & Douma 2004; Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky & Bazerman 2009; Mishina, Dykes, Block & Pollock 2010; Greve, Palmer & Pozner 2010.

**Coding rules.** One category per passage where possible. Code the strain as the actors experienced or communicated it at the time; retrospective claims ("we were under huge pressure") count, but note who is speaking.

## Goals and targets

Include passages that state the specific goals the organization or its units pursued: earnings or EPS targets, sales and market-share goals, prescription or volume targets, launch dates, technical specifications (weight, price, emissions, fuel economy), and "no simulator training" type commitments to customers. Include how ambitious the goals were (stretch targets, public promises to analysts or customers) and who set them. Exclude general statements of corporate mission with no concrete target.

## Blocked legitimate means

Include evidence that the goal could not be reached by legitimate means within the constraints: technical infeasibility, cost ceilings, budget cuts, compressed schedules, missing technology, or regulations that the product could not meet. Include internal acknowledgements that "it can't be done" by the deadline or at the cost, and explicit trade-offs between compliance and cost or time. Exclude constraints that were never linked to the goal in question.

## Competitive and market pressure

Include pressure from rivals (for example Airbus A320neo, competing diesel or opioid products, other energy traders), from analysts, credit-rating agencies and capital markets (stock price, credit rating, earnings expectations), and from market entry or growth ambitions (VW's US diesel push). Include how this pressure was described inside the firm. Exclude neutral descriptions of the industry with no link to the firm's choices.

## Performance shortfall and aspiration gaps

Include passages showing the firm or unit falling short of its own aspirations, past performance or peers, or a high performer struggling to keep up its record: missed quarters, declining sales, losses hidden or feared, slipping schedules, threatened status. Include fears of what would happen if the shortfall became known. Exclude shortfalls that arose only after the wrongdoing was exposed.

## Incentives and rewards tied to targets

Include compensation, bonuses, stock options, sales-force incentive schemes, promotion criteria and performance reviews tied to hitting targets, and sanctions or job threats for missing them. Include evidence on how large or how threshold-dependent these incentives were (for example bonus plans for high-dose OxyContin sales, Enron's "rank and yank"). Exclude incentive structures described only in general terms with no link to the targets or the wrongdoing.

## Pressure transmitted down the hierarchy

Include how senior leaders communicated pressure to subordinates: deadlines framed as non-negotiable, "failure is not an option" language, public reprimands, demands to "find a way," and fear of bringing bad news upward. Include middle managers passing pressure on to engineers, sales staff or traders. Exclude pressure that the passage attributes only to external parties without showing how it moved through the organization.

## Choosing the deviant path

Include the moments where actors chose illegitimate means to achieve the goal (Merton's "innovation"): decisions to install a defeat device, structure an off-balance-sheet deal, defer a safety fix, promote off-label or misrepresent abuse risk, or withhold information from regulators. Include alternatives that were considered and rejected because of cost, time or target pressure, and cost-benefit calculations that weighed compliance against the goal. Exclude later concealment or justification unless it reveals why the original choice was made.
