# Routine activity theory and the opportunity perspective

**Core idea.** Crime occurs when a motivated offender and a suitable target converge in the absence of a capable guardian (Cohen & Felson 1979). Felson later added handlers, who have influence over offenders, and place managers, who are responsible for settings. Benson and Simpson adapted the approach to white-collar crime: offenders have legitimate access to their targets, are spatially separated from victims, and their actions carry a superficial appearance of legitimacy.

**What this lens explains.** Why wrongdoing was possible and why it was not stopped. It shifts attention from offenders' motives to the structure of oversight and opportunity. The guardian-over-time timeline is the most data-ready output.

**Key references.** Cohen & Felson 1979; Felson 1995; Eck 2003; Benson & Simpson 2009/2015; Benson, Madensen & Eck 2009; Clarke 1997.

**Coding rules.** Code who (offender, target, guardian, handler) and when. Keep motive brief here; detailed goal and pressure evidence belongs under strain.

## Motivated offenders and their positions

Include passages identifying who committed or directed the wrongdoing, their organizational position and the access or authority it gave them, and a brief statement of motive. Include the organization itself as offender where the documents treat it so. Exclude extended discussion of pressures and targets (code under strain).

## Suitable targets

Include descriptions of what or who was exploited and what made them attractive or vulnerable: regulators' test procedures, certification processes, investors and creditors, patients and prescribers, consumers, pilots and passengers. Include properties such as value, accessibility, visibility, inability to detect the harm, and dependence on information supplied by the offender. Exclude descriptions of victims' harm after exposure unless they show why the target was suitable.

## Capable guardians and their capacity

Include the external guardians that could have prevented or detected the wrongdoing (regulators such as EPA, CARB, KBA, FAA, FDA, DEA, SEC; auditors; credit-rating agencies; analysts; courts; independent testers) and evidence of their resources, expertise, mandate, information access and actual oversight practices. Exclude internal controls (code under handlers and place managers).

## Weakened, captured or delegated guardianship

Include evidence that guardianship was absent, weakened or absorbed by the offender: delegation of oversight to the firm (FAA's ODA), auditors with consulting conflicts, regulators lacking staff or expertise, gaps in standards (no rear-impact fuel standard), lobbying to shape oversight, revolving doors, and guardians relying on data supplied by the firm. Exclude general complaints about regulation with no link to the case.

## Predictable guardian routines

Include passages showing that offenders knew and exploited the timing, procedures or blind spots of oversight: emission test cycles detectable by software, audit sampling, certification paths that would not examine a feature, reporting thresholds, and quarter-end timing. Include evidence that the wrongdoing was designed around these routines. Exclude descriptions of procedures with no indication they were exploited.

## Handlers and place managers (internal controls)

Include internal parties with influence over offenders or responsibility for the setting: supervisors, boards and board committees, compliance, legal, internal audit, ethics officers, risk management, safety engineers and colleagues positioned to intervene. Code both their presence and their failure or success to act. Exclude external regulators and auditors (code under guardians).

## Legitimate access and separation from victims

Include evidence that offenders reached their targets through legitimate occupational roles and routine business activity (certification submissions, financial reporting, sales calls, product design), and that they were spatially, temporally or socially distant from those harmed. Exclude access gained through plainly illegitimate means such as theft.

## Superficial legitimacy and concealment

Include techniques that gave wrongdoing the look of normal, lawful business: compliant-looking paperwork, legal and accounting opinions, misleading but technically accurate disclosures, complexity used to deter scrutiny, document destruction, and false statements to guardians. Exclude internal rationalizations unless they were used to deceive guardians or targets.

## Convergence breaks and detection

Include how and when the opportunity structure changed and the wrongdoing came to light: unscheduled or new guardians (WVU road tests, journalists, plaintiffs), whistleblowers, short sellers, crashes or accidents, new standards or enforcement, and how guardians' behavior changed afterwards. Include the dates. Exclude later legal proceedings unless they describe the detection itself.
