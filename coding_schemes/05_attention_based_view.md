# Attention-based view of the firm

**Core idea.** What an organization does depends on what its structure directs decision makers to attend to (Ocasio 1997). Attention is situated (it depends on the context of decisions) and structurally distributed (it flows through communication channels, procedures, rules of the game, players' positions and resources). Applied to wrongdoing, the lens explains a pattern found across these cases: warnings existed but did not register, or registered in a form that produced no corrective action.

**What this lens explains.** Why wrongdoing was not stopped from inside: whistleblowing and warnings as a matter of structure and attention, not only individual courage.

**Key references.** Ocasio 1997, 2011; Ocasio & Joseph 2005; Ocasio, Laamanen & Vaara 2018; Hoffman & Ocasio 2001; Vaughan 1996 (for weak signals).

**Coding rules.** Track each warning as an episode where possible: what the signal was, who raised it, through which channel, who received it, and what happened next. One passage may describe several elements; code the dominant one.

## Warning signals and anomalies

Include issues that could have drawn attention to the wrongdoing or its risks: internal memos and emails raising concerns, test results, field data, crash or incident reports, customer complaints, abuse and diversion reports, audit findings, analyst or press questions, and whistleblower reports. Include who raised the signal and when. Exclude signals that arose only after public exposure.

## Communication and procedural channels

Include the channels through which signals travelled or were supposed to travel: reporting lines, meetings, committees, board and audit committee procedures, escalation processes, hotlines, certification documentation, regulatory submissions, and informal routes. Include evidence on how these channels were designed, bypassed or blocked. Exclude organization charts with no link to information flow.

## Structural positions and players

Include evidence on who held which position relative to the issue: who knew, who decided, who had authority to act, and the distance between them (hierarchical, functional, geographic). Include the roles of specific players such as Watkins, Lay, engineers, test pilots, recall coordinators, compliance staff and board members. Exclude biographical information unrelated to the flow of attention.

## Rules of the game and attention-directing incentives

Include the formal and informal rules, metrics and incentives that told people what mattered: KPIs on schedule, cost, sales or earnings; performance reviews; reporting templates; and norms about what could be raised with superiors. Include evidence that these rules made some issues salient and others invisible. Exclude incentives described without any link to what people noticed or prioritized (code under strain).

## Focus of attention and dominant agendas

Include passages showing what decision makers actually attended to: the issues and answers that dominated meetings, board minutes, executive communications and strategy documents (earnings, launch dates, market share, cost targets, competitor moves), especially at times when warning signals were present. Exclude routine business discussion with no link to the period or issues under study.

## Filtering, discounting and neglect of warnings

Include how warnings were dismissed, reframed, narrowed or routed away: narrowly scoped investigations (Vinson & Elkins), cognitive scripts that screened out cases (recall coordinators), reassurances accepted without checking, delegation of the issue to people without authority, and signals that never reached decision makers. Exclude deliberate cover-ups aimed at outsiders unless they also kept the issue from internal decision makers.

## Organizational responses and moves

Include the actions taken (or not taken) in response to signals: investigations launched, fixes deferred, recalls delayed, redesigns, disclosures, statements to regulators, retaliation against messengers, and their timing relative to the signal. Exclude legal proceedings after exposure unless they reveal the internal response.

## Attention shifts and triggering events

Include events that redirected organizational attention: crashes, deaths, media exposés, regulator letters, test results from outsiders, short-seller reports, lawsuits, and leadership changes. Include evidence of how agendas, channels or priorities changed afterwards. Exclude events with no observable effect on attention or response.
