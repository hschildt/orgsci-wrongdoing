# Moral disengagement and neutralization

**Core idea.** People who take part in wrongdoing usually keep a moral self-image. They do so by switching off self-sanction for specific acts. Sykes and Matza (1957) described the "techniques of neutralization" offenders use to excuse what they do. Bandura (1999) set out eight mechanisms of moral disengagement. Anand, Ashforth and Joshi (2004) showed how organizational members use such rationalizations to sustain corruption.

**What this lens explains.** How individuals live with wrongdoing: the accounts, language and framing that let them act against their own standards. It gives the most text-ready dataset, since it works directly on what people said and wrote.

**Key references.** Sykes & Matza 1957; Bandura 1999; Bandura, Caprara & Zsolnai 2000; Anand, Ashforth & Joshi 2004; Moore, Detert, Treviño, Baker & Mayer 2012; Bauman 1989.

**Coding rules.** Code the words and reasoning of participants: testimony, emails, memos, internal presentations, sales scripts, quoted statements in complaints and statements of facts. A prosecutor's or investigator's own characterization counts only when it reports what participants said or believed. Code the most prominent mechanism; a passage may be coded twice if two mechanisms are clearly present.

## Moral justification and appeal to higher loyalties

Include accounts that present the conduct as serving a worthy purpose or a higher loyalty: helping patients in pain, protecting jobs, serving shareholders, defending the company, beating a foreign competitor, advancing innovation or clean diesel, loyalty to the team or the boss. Exclude neutral statements of corporate mission that are not offered as a reason for the conduct in question.

## Euphemistic labeling

Include sanitized, technical or convoluted language that disguises what was done: "acoustic function," "test recognition," "prepay," "monetize," "structured transaction," "titration," "pseudoaddiction," "speed bump," and similar terms used by participants. Include passages where the plain description was deliberately avoided or banned. Exclude technical terms used only in their ordinary technical sense.

## Advantageous comparison

Include comparisons that make the conduct look benign: "competitors do worse," "everyone in the industry does this," comparisons with more harmful alternatives (other opioids, other emission strategies, rival aircraft), and claims that the conduct was minor relative to other risks. Exclude factual industry comparisons not used to excuse the conduct.

## Displacement of responsibility

Include accounts that place responsibility on authorities or experts: following orders from superiors, relying on approval by lawyers, auditors, boards or regulators ("Andersen signed off," "the FAA approved it," "the label was FDA-approved"), and claims that the decision was made "above my level." Exclude factual descriptions of approval chains not used to deflect responsibility.

## Diffusion of responsibility and denial of personal involvement

Include accounts in which responsibility is spread across a group, process or division of labor ("it was a team decision," "the system," "no one person"), and denials of knowledge or involvement ("I didn't know," "I wasn't told," "a couple of software engineers," "rogue employees"). Include scapegoating of low-level employees. Exclude credible factual evidence about who knew what, which belongs to other lenses, unless it is offered as an excuse.

## Distortion or denial of consequences

Include minimizing, ignoring, disputing or misconstruing the harm: claims that no one was really hurt, that emissions were negligible, that abuse rates were "less than one percent," that the risk was statistically trivial, that investors were sophisticated, or that the deaths and injuries were caused by something else. Include cost-benefit framings that reduce harm to an abstract number. Exclude neutral technical risk estimates not used to downplay harm.

## Blaming and dehumanizing victims

Include accounts that blame those harmed or strip them of moral standing: "abusers" and "criminal addicts," pilots who should have "followed procedures," drivers' behavior, investors who "should have read the footnotes," and depersonalized references to victims as statistics or cases. Exclude neutral descriptions of user or operator behavior not used to shift blame.

## Condemnation of the condemners

Include attacks on the motives, competence or legitimacy of critics, regulators, journalists, short sellers, plaintiffs' lawyers or whistleblowers: claims that critics are biased, ignorant, self-interested or hypocritical, and dismissal of the rules themselves as unreasonable. Exclude substantive factual disagreement with critics that does not question their standing.

## Appeal to legality and technical compliance

Include accounts that treat formal or technical compliance as settling the moral question: "it was legal," "the accounting was GAAP-compliant," "we met the standard," "the test procedure allowed it," and bureaucratic framings that turn moral questions into technical ones (Bauman's adiaphorization, such as pricing a death in a cost-benefit memo). Exclude statements of the legal position made by neutral parties.

## Metaphor of the ledger

Include accounts in which past good deeds, contributions or loyalty are presented as earning the right to an occasional transgression or as offsetting it: philanthropy, safety record, years of service, value created for shareholders, charitable giving by the owners. Exclude descriptions of good deeds not linked to the wrongdoing.
