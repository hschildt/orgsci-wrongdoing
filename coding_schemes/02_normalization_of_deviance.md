# Normalization of deviance and corruption

**Core idea.** Wrongdoing rarely begins as a decision to do wrong. It grows through small deviations that are accepted, redefined as normal and built into routines. Vaughan (1996) showed this in NASA's decision to launch Challenger. Ashforth and Anand (2003) describe three mutually reinforcing pillars of normalized corruption: institutionalization, rationalization and socialization. Palmer (2012) treats wrongdoing as a normal product of ordinary organizational processes.

**What this lens explains.** Persistence and spread: how a practice lasts for years and involves many people with no single villain. Code the processes that made deviance routine, not the original motive (strain) or individual self-justification (moral disengagement).

**Key references.** Vaughan 1996, 1999; Ashforth & Anand 2003; Anand, Ashforth & Joshi 2004; Brief, Buttram & Dukerich 2001; Palmer 2012; Pinto, Leana & Pil 2008.

**Coding rules.** Look for sequence and time: earlier and later versions, repeated approvals, newcomers joining. A single wrongful act with no evidence of repetition or embedding usually belongs to another lens.

## Incremental drift and escalation

Include passages that show wrongdoing developing in small steps, each a modest extension of the last accepted practice: successive software versions, a series of ever-larger or riskier deals (LJM to the Raptors), MCAS authority growing between design iterations, higher doses and broader indications promoted over time. Include escalation of commitment, where earlier steps make later ones seem necessary. Exclude one-off decisions with no developmental sequence.

## Redefinition of acceptable risk and standards

Include evidence that technical, legal, accounting or safety standards were reinterpreted so that deviations counted as acceptable: anomalies classed as "within experience," test failures treated as non-issues, accounting rules read as permitting the structure, abuse reframed as "pseudoaddiction," a hazard classified as "major" rather than "catastrophic." Include who did the redefining and on what grounds. Exclude passages that only state the formal standard without showing it being reinterpreted.

## Institutionalization in routines, structures and technology

Include passages where the practice is embedded in formal procedures, organizational units, budgets, software code, product designs, sales training materials, deal templates or approval workflows, so that it continues regardless of who is in post. Include evidence that the practice survived turnover or spread across product lines, models or business units. Exclude informal one-time arrangements.

## Collective rationalizations and shared vocabulary

Include shared accounts, ideologies and terms that made the practice seem legitimate across a group: euphemisms in common use ("acoustic function," "cycle beating," "structured finance," "titration"), collective beliefs ("everyone in the industry does this," "regulators will never test it that way"), and cultural narratives that framed rule-bending as smart or necessary. Code group-level or organizational discourse here; code a single person's self-justification under moral disengagement. Exclude vocabulary used only by outside critics.

## Socialization and co-optation of newcomers

Include how new employees, managers, contractors or partners were drawn into the practice: training that taught the deviant practice as standard, gradual involvement ("just help with this one part"), co-optation through rewards or status, mentoring by participants, and compromise when newcomers raised concerns. Include those who joined later in the timeline (new engineers, new sales reps, new board members, banks invited into LJM). Exclude general onboarding with no link to the practice.

## Structural secrecy and fragmented knowledge

Include evidence that specialization, division of labor, information silos, compartmentalization, confidentiality rules or physical and organizational distance kept people from seeing the whole picture: engineers who knew only their module, board members who saw only summaries, regulators who received partial information, legal or audit reviews scoped narrowly. Include deliberate need-to-know restrictions. Exclude deliberate concealment from outsiders after the fact unless it also fragmented internal knowledge.

## Leadership tolerance and cultural endorsement

Include passages where senior leaders, boards, auditors or lawyers tolerated, approved, waived rules for or rewarded the deviant practice, signalling that it was acceptable: ethics-code waivers, approvals without scrutiny, praise for those who "delivered," sanctions for those who objected, and cultures that put production, schedule or growth first. Exclude leadership actions taken after the wrongdoing became public.
